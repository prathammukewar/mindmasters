How a session works

Ten problems, picked for exactly where you are.

1

Tap Train

The app looks at your rating and pulls ten problems you have not solved yet, from every topic in the arena, matched to your level. A session is never too easy or too hard.

2

Answer, then see the real solution

Every contest problem shows its official answer and links to the full worked solution on the AoPS wiki. Miss one and it comes back tomorrow in Smart Review.

3

Watch the rating move

Math and chess each have their own rating. Beat a hard problem and it jumps. The profile keeps a chart of every rated game, so a kid can see the line go up.

4

Come back tomorrow

A streak, a daily challenge, monthly seasons, badges, and a mystery chest that only opens after the day's checklist. The same loop that keeps adults on language apps, aimed at contest prep.

The home screen

One place for today.

The Today card lists what unlocks the chest. Below it, each arena has a Train button and a running count of everything solved so far.

Progress you can see

A line that goes up.

Ratings work like chess ratings: an 800 is a beginner, a 1400 is a strong middle schooler, and every solve moves the number a little. Kids check it the way adults check a step counter.

For parents

Safe to hand to a nine-year-old.

  • Nothing leaves the device. There is no sign-up, no server, no analytics, and no ads. Progress is saved in the browser on that device.
  • Real problems only. Every math problem is an actual past contest problem with its official answer. Nothing is invented.
  • Install it like an app. On an iPad or iPhone, open the site in Safari, tap Share, then Add to Home Screen. On a Chromebook or laptop, Chrome offers Install in the address bar.
  • Keep a backup. Because progress lives on one device, the profile screen makes a backup code that can be restored on another device or after a browser reset.
  • Several kids, one iPad. Each child gets their own account and, if they want one, a password.

For coaches and teachers

A class, without the admin.

  • Assignments by code. Build a set from any topic or contest and hand students a short code. They paste it in and play.
  • Results come back the same way. Students send a submission code. It fills your roster, marks missing work, and shows which problem tripped the most kids.
  • Reports you can print. A class report and per-student pages, plus CSV export for your own spreadsheet.
  • Optional live leaderboard. A free Firebase Class Cloud, set up from inside the app in a few minutes, keeps a class scoreboard live.
  • Practice tests with real timing. 191 full contests with official scoring, so a Saturday mock exam takes no preparation.

Questions

Things people ask.

Is it really free?

Yes. There is no paid tier, no trial, and no upsell. It is a single web page that runs in the browser.

Which contests are covered?

Every AMC 8 from 1999 to 2025, every AMC 10 and AMC 12 from 2000 to 2025, and every AIME from 1983 to 2025, with official answers. The topic arenas draw on the same problems, sorted by subject, plus school-level practice for younger students. Chess covers openings, tactics, endgames and strategy, with puzzles rated from beginner to grandmaster.

Where is my child's progress saved?

In the browser on the device they use. Nothing is uploaded anywhere. If they switch devices or the browser is reset, the profile screen makes a backup code that restores everything.

Does it work without internet?

Yes. After the first visit the whole app is stored on the device, and once it is installed on the home screen it opens like any other app, connection or not.

Can I use it with a class?

Yes. Create a teacher account, build an assignment, and share the code. Students do not need accounts on any server; results come back as codes or, optionally, through a free class leaderboard you set up yourself.

Who made this?

Pratham Mukewar, who coaches North South Foundation math and CheckMates chess. It started as a tool for his own students. The code is public on GitHub.

MindMasters Academy is free to use. AMC and AIME problems are copyright of the Mathematical Association of America and appear here for practice. Chess puzzle positions are drawn from public game records.
